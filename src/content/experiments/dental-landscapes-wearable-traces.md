## Starting point / 灵感记录

**Proposed / Planned — idea only, not yet fabricated.** Recorded on **6 November 2026**. No prototype, cutting test, wearable trial or material photograph is being presented as completed work.

“牙套盒牙齿起起伏伏，或许可以做成‘立体山水画’？亦或是剪裁成一颗一颗牙，做成戒指？”

The starting observation is the rise and fall of the dental arch held in an old transparent aligner. The aligner itself, and possibly its box or an available dental mould/model, could become material for a new form. Access to suitable boxes, moulds and models still needs to be confirmed.

## Direction A — miniature landscape / 微缩立体山水、地形画

An intact aligner's cusps, hollows and curved arch could read as ridges, valleys and a miniature terrain. Several retired stages might be arranged as layered relief in a shallow frame or box. A box could act as a viewing chamber; a model or replica could help explore positive and negative forms, if available.

Transparency, spacing, side lighting and shadows are possible tests, not established results. The composition could evoke 山水 through depth, empty space and shifting viewpoints without pretending to reproduce a geographical place.

**The conceptual move is from bodily contour to landscape.** Something originally designed to guide a private bodily change becomes a terrain that can be looked across. If treatment stages remain identifiable, their gradual differences could suggest the body's repeated remaking over time. A beautiful miniature alone may hide the labour of treatment, so the test is whether a restrained stage/date label or a short sequence can retain that history.

## Direction B — tooth-shaped jewellery / 单牙轮廓戒指等个人物件

A separate proposal is to trace or cut around individual tooth compartments and explore them as ring tops, pendants or small brooch elements. The hollow tooth form could remain recognisable, or become an ambiguous transparent setting. An initial replica could be mounted on a separate jewellery base and evaluated as a display maquette before any wear is considered.

**The conceptual move is from body-related waste to a wearable personal object.** An aligner is a manufactured trace of a body, not an extracted tooth or bodily tissue. A discarded treatment tool might acquire the intimacy of a keepsake: a record of a previous dental shape and of time spent maintaining it.

Moving that trace from inside the mouth to an externally visible ornament also changes who can see it. It could express ownership, pride, discomfort or ambivalence. These are questions for the work, not assumed audience responses. An actual used object and a clean replica would carry different meanings; their provenance should be stated honestly.

## Relation to Cost of Smile

- **Economic cost:** a paid, serial treatment object becomes obsolete as the body moves to another stage. Turning it into a landscape or ornament asks how medical purchase, disposability and personal value coexist. Jewellery does not recover the treatment fee, and no resale value is claimed.
- **Personal / bodily cost:** the retained shell can stand for wear time, cleaning, discomfort and repeated self-management. Terrain translates that history into space; a keepsake translates it into a personal attachment.
- **Social cost:** a tool associated with improving one's smile becomes visible decoration. Would the object question beauty expectations, or reproduce them by making treatment residue desirable? What changes when a private routine becomes something others inspect?
- **Body as project:** each aligner belongs to a planned bodily stage. Reframing its contours as terrain or jewellery could interrupt that forward-looking programme and let an earlier, supposedly superseded state remain meaningful.

These are working interpretations within the existing [three-cost framework](/CostOfSmile/research/three-cost-framework/) and [body-as-project notes](/CostOfSmile/research/body-as-project-working-framework/), not findings from a completed experiment.

## Material and safety limits / 实施限制

**The polymer is unconfirmed.** Clear appearance does not identify a material. PETG, TPU, copolyesters and multilayer constructions occur in aligner products, with differences between brands and product lines. Erkodent's documentation, for example, distinguishes PET-G Erkodur and PET-G/TPU Erkoloc-pro; this does not identify the material of my own aligners. Check the exact product documentation before choosing a process. [Material source](https://www.erkodent.de/wp-content/documents/products/alignerinfo_EN.pdf)

Cutting can create sharp edges, cracks, fragments or layer separation. Erkoloc-pro's manufacturer specifically warns that incorrect processing can cause cracks, breaks and sharp edges. Its dental instructions do **not** establish that a modified object is safe as jewellery. [Manufacturer instructions](https://www.erkodent.de/wp-content/documents/products/Erkoloc-pro_multi.pdf)

For this proposed experiment:

- Begin with documented, cleaned retired aligners for **non-worn display studies**, or use unused alternative transparent material or replicas. Do not cut an appliance still needed for treatment.
- Cleaning is not evidence of sterilisation or of suitability for prolonged skin contact. **Do not directly wear untreated objects that have contacted the mouth.** A cleaned used aligner is not automatically a safe wearable either.
- Confirm a material-compatible cleaning method from the relevant manufacturer; do not assume that heat, solvents, adhesives or coatings are suitable for an unidentified plastic.
- Assess edges, fracture, attachment security, hygiene and skin-contact suitability before considering wear. Prefer a suitable replica and a purpose-made jewellery base for an initial wearable study; those materials and fixings still require assessment.
- Keep cut fragments secured and inaccessible to casual handling. A stable mount or enclosed display may be more appropriate than a touchable object. Do not return modified pieces to the mouth.

This is an artistic proposal, not a dental reuse or fabrication guide. Display safety and wearable safety are separate decisions.

## References verified on 6 November 2026

These are **conceptual precedents**, not evidence that someone has already made this exact aligner landscape or ring, and not instructions for reusing dental appliances. The connections below are my proposed readings.

1. **Ana Mendieta, Silueta series (1973–80).** MoMA describes bodily imprints and silhouettes in natural landscapes. This suggests a way to think about bodily form as a trace within terrain. My miniature dental landscape would differ in scale, material and context from Mendieta's work. [MoMA artist record](https://www.moma.org/collection/artists/3924)
2. **Shaun Leane for Alexander McQueen, “Spine” Corset, spring/summer 1998.** The Met documents an aluminium and leather object that brings skeletal structure to the body's exterior. It offers a reference for turning anatomy into adornment; it is not made from used aligners or biological waste. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://blog.metmuseum.org/alexandermcqueen/spine-corset-untitled/)
3. **Marc Quinn, Self (series begun 1991).** In a Yale Center for British Art interview, Quinn discusses repeated self-portraits made using his blood and their dependence on refrigeration. The relevant question here is how material can carry bodily identity and time. An aligner is manufactured residue, unlike blood; this is not a proposal to imitate Quinn's material or preservation process. [Yale Center for British Art interview](https://britishart.yale.edu/interview-marc-quinn)

## Planned next steps and documentation

1. Inventory retired aligners and any available boxes/models; record product, treatment stage and condition where known.
2. Photograph the actual objects before altering anything. Material photographs can be added later through Pages CMS; **no generated or substitute image is presented as documentation**.
3. Make two distinct non-worn studies: an intact contour/landscape arrangement and a single-tooth replica/jewellery maquette.
4. Compare recognition, translucency, stability and how clearly each form communicates bodily history. Record failures as well as useful results.
5. Decide whether either direction merits fabrication only after the material and safety questions are resolved.
