## Starting point

The idea appeared while eating pasta with a pale soy-milk-based sauce.

During clear-aligner treatment, I have gradually developed an informal sense that some foods feel more **“aligner-friendly”** than others. This is interesting precisely because Invisalign aligners are normally removed for eating: the classification is not really about an official diet. It is about the **maintenance calculations that treatment makes me perform around eating**.

My first two intuitive creative variables were:

- **colour / transparency** — pale or clear foods feel visually less threatening to the idea of keeping an orthodontic system “clean”;
- **sugar level** — sugar becomes one of the things I mentally calculate because eating can lead to another cleaning decision before the aligners go back in.

These are starting observations, not clinical rankings.

## Variables to test rather than assume

If this develops, I should document actual meals and test which dimensions remain meaningful:

- **staining / visible residue** — what actually leaves colour or material behind;
- **cleaning burden** — how much rinsing, brushing or checking I feel compelled to do;
- **hardness / attachment risk** — especially because I have previously detached a small orthodontic attachment while eating;
- **extra cleaning** — whether a food creates an additional action that would not otherwise happen;
- **spontaneous-eating friction** — how much the remove → eat → clean → reinsert routine discourages an unplanned snack or changes when I decide to eat;
- **wear-time interruption** — how eating becomes a timing calculation because the aligners are temporarily out.

The point is to observe a lived system of self-management before deciding which categories belong in the final artwork.

## Possible visual forms

### 1. White → dark spectrum

Foods could be arranged from transparent / white to strongly coloured.

The chart could resemble a dental shade guide, but instead of ranking tooth colour it maps everyday meals. This would turn a familiar visual language of dentistry toward mundane eating decisions.

### 2. Multi-axis scoring

A food could be plotted across several **experiential** variables rather than given one authoritative score:

- colour intensity;
- perceived sugar;
- residue / staining;
- hardness;
- cleaning effort;
- extra cleaning;
- interruption to wear time;
- spontaneous-eating friction.

The result could become a matrix, radar chart, colour field, card system, or deliberately over-engineered “clinical” classification interface.

### 3. A menu for an orthodontic body

A fictional menu could be organised not by cuisine, price or taste but by how much **maintenance** each food seems to create.

This makes eating visible as another way treatment reorganises ordinary behaviour.

### 4. Personal food log → visual taxonomy

Rather than inventing a complete chart immediately, I could first log real meals for several days. Each meal would generate small observations about colour, residue, cleaning and interruption. The final visual system would then grow from actual lived data instead of generic assumptions.

## Relation to Personal / Bodily Cost

This idea belongs most clearly to **personal / bodily cost**.

The “cost” is not only discomfort. It can be the accumulation of tiny behavioural negotiations:

**Can I eat this now? Do I want to take the aligners out? Will I need to brush again? Is this snack worth another interruption?**

A food chart can therefore make the project more personal without relying on private screenshots or direct clinical imagery. It converts a daily inconvenience into an aesthetic system.

There is also humour in treating ordinary pasta, snacks and drinks with the seriousness of a medical classification chart. That tension may help the work avoid becoming only a complaint.

## Important caution

This is a **personal artistic taxonomy**, not dental, medical or nutritional advice.

Official Invisalign guidance describes the aligners as removable for eating and drinking, so I should not present the artwork as a formal list of “safe” and “unsafe” foods. If I later make factual claims about sugar, staining, acidity, oral health or attachment damage, those claims need separate reliable research.

For now, the strongest material is the lived friction itself: **how treatment changes the way I think before, during and after eating**.
