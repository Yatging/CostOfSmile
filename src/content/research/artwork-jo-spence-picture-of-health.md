## Work / medium

Jo Spence's **The Picture of Health?** (1982–1986) developed from photographic self-portraiture made after her breast-cancer diagnosis. The Women's Art Collection describes the series as both a challenge to idealised images of the female body and a form of “phototherapy” through which Spence sought to regain agency in relation to medical authority.

## What the work does

The photographs refuse a detached clinical viewpoint. The person undergoing treatment becomes an active image-maker rather than a passive case.

The work is therefore useful not because it documents illness, but because it asks **who controls the representation of the treated body**.

## Why it matters to Cost of Smile

My first instinct was to include progress photographs as evidence. Spence shows that personal medical imagery can do more when it is deliberately authored, staged, annotated or reframed.

If I use treatment photographs at all, the question should not be “does this prove my teeth changed?” but “what kind of image of the treated self am I constructing?”

## Formal method to borrow

- reclaim medical documentation through self-representation;
- make personal experience visible without pretending to clinical neutrality;
- combine body, text and photographic framing;
- use authorship itself as part of the critique.

## Caution

Spence's work emerges from cancer, feminism and serious illness. Orthodontic treatment cannot be compared in severity. The relevant lesson is the politics of **representation and agency**, not the medical condition itself.
